\begin{abstract}
Parameter-efficient fine-tuning (PEFT) methods such as low-rank adaptation (LoRA)
freeze a pre-trained backbone and train few parameters, which suits continual learning
on resource-constrained devices. However, sequentially fine-tuning a shared LoRA
adapter still forgets catastrophically: the low-rank subspace is overwritten by new
tasks. We propose \textbf{FOLoRA} (Fisher-Orthogonalized Low-Rank Adaptation), a
replay-free, fixed-budget method protecting the directions past tasks deem important.
FOLoRA maintains, per adapted layer, an accumulated second-order importance kernel from
the empirical Fisher restricted to the adapter parameters. Its top eigendirections form
an \emph{importance-weighted protected subspace}; during training a regularizer
penalizes alignment of the adapter displacement with them, weighted by eigenvalue.
This yields a stability--plasticity trade-off: important directions are preserved,
unimportant ones stay reusable. We further derive a second-order forgetting bound with
an explicit higher-order remainder, and show FOLoRA's regularizer minimizes its leading
term. We audit that bound's slack empirically, using it as a design principle for
\emph{what} to penalize rather than a predictor of \emph{how much} it helps. FOLoRA
matches the strongest regularized baseline on Split CIFAR-100 and Split ImageNet-R
under the frozen-feature nearest-class-mean protocol. Unlike per-task-adapter methods
(O-LoRA, InfLoRA), FOLoRA needs neither replay nor parameter growth with task count
($\sim18\times$ fewer trainable parameters) and adds no inference cost, as the update
merges into the backbone. We also characterize the trade-off: a rank-$k$ importance
model rather than EWC's full diagonal costs a larger footprint, which we quantify
alongside accuracy.
\end{abstract}
