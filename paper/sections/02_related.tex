\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Continual learning}
Classical continual learning methods fall into three families. Rehearsal methods store
and replay a subset of past data~\cite{rebuffi2017icarl}; regularization methods add a
penalty that slows down learning on parameters important to past tasks, most notably EWC
via the diagonal Fisher information~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} and SI/MAS via
synaptic importance~\cite{zenke2017continual,aljundi2018memory}; dynamic methods grow the
network per task~\cite{rusu2016progressive,mallya2018packnet}. Recent surveys of the
field appear in~\cite{leo2024survey,razavifar2026harsh,cossu2026streaming}, and the
exemplar-free, representation-level, and distillation-based variants most relevant here
in~\cite{li2025exemplarfree,ferdinand2025featureexp,liu2026diverse,carta2024pld}. Our method is a
regularization method, but it operates on the low-rank subspace of a frozen backbone
rather than on raw parameters.

\paragraph{Parameter-efficient continual learning with pre-trained models}
A recent and effective line of work keeps a pre-trained backbone frozen and learns only
prompts or adapters; the space is
surveyed in~\cite{zhou2024ptmsurvey,zhou2025revisitingcil,coleman2026peftsurvey,yang2025foundationsurvey}.
Prompt-based methods --- L2P~\cite{wang2022l2p},
DualPrompt~\cite{wang2022dualprompt}, and CODA-Prompt~\cite{smith2023coda} --- learn a
pool of prompts and select or compose them per input. LoRA-based methods attach low-rank
adapters. Related designs differ in where they spend the per-task budget: expandable or
branched subspaces~\cite{zhou2024ease,he2025cllora,zhu2025bilora,wang2025plan}, decoupled
scaling rules~\cite{wu2025sdlora}, orthogonal adapter banks~\cite{fang2026olorabanks},
further adapter variants~\cite{chen2024cat,li2026adapterx,wang2025adapters,luo2025eratdlora},
dual-consolidation and hierarchical prompt
decompositions~\cite{zhou2025duct,wang2023hideprompt}, and prototype- or prompt-routing
schemes~\cite{pan2026prototype,xi2025pkenet,rebillard2025cvm,zhao2027talon}; for the online
and task-free regime, see~\cite{wei2025onlinelora}.

\paragraph{Orthogonalization and subspace allocation}
A distinct line makes \emph{where} a new task may write the design variable, rather than
how strongly. Its classical form is gradient projection: orthogonal gradient
descent~\cite{farajtabar2018orthogonal}, GPM~\cite{saha2021gradient} and the
null-space-of-feature-covariance update of Adam-NSCL~\cite{wang2021adamnsc} retain a basis
describing past tasks and constrain new updates to its orthogonal complement, a
construction that also admits generalization guarantees~\cite{bennani2019generalisation}.
The same idea reappears in PEFT as subspace allocation for low-rank adapters:
O-LoRA~\cite{wang2023olora} orthogonally initializes each task's adapter against previous
tasks' input subspaces, and InfLoRA~\cite{liang2024inflora} places the new task's subspace
in the intersection of the new task's gradient space and the orthogonal complement of the
old tasks' gradient space, removing interference while retaining plasticity. Recent
variants move the per-task budget along this axis --- expandable or branched
subspaces~\cite{zhou2024ease,he2025cllora,zhu2025bilora,wang2025plan} and null-space
constructions for prompt tuning~\cite{lu2024nsp2}.

Two properties are shared across this line, and they are what FOLoRA changes. First, the
protected subspace is obtained from a \emph{structural} criterion --- the past tasks'
input or feature covariance, a gradient subspace, or an explicit orthogonality constraint
--- rather than from the curvature of the past loss. Second, within the protected subspace
every retained direction is defended equally: the budget decides \emph{how many}
directions to keep, never \emph{how much} each one matters. FOLoRA keeps the subspace
framing and replaces both choices with a single object, the adapter block of the
accumulated empirical Fisher, so that the retained directions are those in which the past
loss is most curved and each is penalized in proportion to its curvature. The equal-weight
geometry of this family is then the special case $\sigma_j\equiv1$ of FOLoRA's penalty
(Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}), and we compare against O-LoRA and InfLoRA empirically in
Sec.~\ref{sec:main_results}.

\paragraph{Second-order methods and curvature-weighted penalties}
EWC~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming} uses the diagonal Fisher as a per-parameter
importance weight. FOLoRA combines that idea with the subspace framing above: it uses the
top-$k$ spectrum of the adapter block of the second-order expansion --- not the diagonal
Fisher, and not the full $d\times d$ matrix either --- and applies it as a soft,
importance-weighted orthogonal projection rather than a hard null-space constraint, which
avoids the rank saturation that hard projections suffer once the null space shrinks.
Recent analyses of flatness-aware optimization approach the same goal from other
angles~\cite{yang2025flatnessogp}, flatness itself has been connected to generalization
bounds~\cite{liu2025flatloss}, and gradient-norm regularization acts on the same
stability--plasticity axis without a curvature weighting~\cite{wang2026gnr}.

\paragraph{Theory of forgetting}
Second-order analyses of forgetting are well established for models trained from scratch:
the forgetting of a task is governed by the curvature of its loss landscape and by the
overlap between successive tasks' input distributions or NTKs~\cite{nguyen2019toward,
evron2022catastrophic,karakida2022learning,doan2021theoretical}. We build on this line
but instantiate it in the low-rank adapter subspace of a frozen pre-trained model, and we
use it constructively: the leading term of the bound is exactly the quantity that a
subspace regularizer can minimize, which yields the FOLoRA objective
(Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}). Recent theory sharpens this picture for lazy-training and kernel
regimes~\cite{graldi2025lazy,zheng2026ntk}, and the empirical counterpart --- that
low-rank adaptation forgets in a measurably different way from full
fine-tuning~\cite{shuttleworth2025intruder} --- is part of what motivates working in the
adapter subspace rather than in the full parameter space.

\paragraph{Low-rank adaptation}
LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} approximates weight updates by low-rank matrices and has become a
standard PEFT primitive. Subsequent work studies initialization, rank allocation, and
merging~\cite{luo2025eratdlora,li2026adapterx}. We build directly on LoRA and extend it
with a second-order anti-forgetting mechanism specialized for continual learning.

\paragraph{Evaluation protocols in PEFT-based continual learning}
Results in this setting are strongly protocol-dependent. Keeping the standard trainable
classification head that grows with the task sequence, class-incremental accuracy is
typically in the single digits even when task-incremental accuracy is above 90\%, because
the loss is dominated by the classifier rather than by the adapted features; replacing the
head at evaluation time with a nearest-class-mean classifier over frozen features raises
the same checkpoints into the 70--80\% range. Two consequences matter for how a method
should be tuned and compared. First, hyperparameters --- the regularization strength in
particular --- are only identifiable relative to the protocol under which they were
selected, and an optimum found under a classifier-dominated metric need not be the optimum
under the reported one. Second, a ranking established under one protocol does not transfer
to another. Accordingly, all defaults reported in this paper are re-selected under the
frozen-feature nearest-class-mean protocol, and every comparison we draw is made within
that protocol. That the protocol can dominate the reported conclusion is now being
recognized independently: recent work shows the class-incremental average is sensitive to
evaluation choices and can misrepresent representation
quality~\cite{lai2026lieaverage,cha2025diverseeval,lee2026mega}, that classifier bias and
calibration distort comparisons unless handled
explicitly~\cite{pomponi2025dampening,zhang2026confidence}, and that simple
frozen-feature classifiers over a pre-trained backbone remain competitive with more
elaborate designs~\cite{mcdonnell2023ranpac,castellana2026cdimm,tran2026convexhull}.

\paragraph{Gap addressed in this work}
None of the lines above supplies what this paper sets out to provide. Subspace-based
methods \cite{wang2023olora,liang2024inflora,wang2021adamnsc} protect a past task's
subspace as a whole, so every retained direction is defended with the same strength
regardless of how much the past loss actually depends on it, and most of them allocate a
branch per task, so the trainable parameter count grows with the sequence length.
Second-order analyses of forgetting \cite{nguyen2019toward,doan2021theoretical}
characterize \emph{why} forgetting happens but are developed for models trained from
scratch and are not instantiated in a fixed low-rank adapter, nor do they yield an
objective one can simply train with. What is missing is a
regularizer that is \emph{derived} from a forgetting bound carrying an explicit remainder,
that weights protected directions by their curvature rather than uniformly, and that keeps
the parameter budget constant across tasks. The derivation is used here as a statement of
\emph{which} quantity to penalize rather than as a numerical prediction of \emph{how much}
forgetting a setting removes: Sec.~\ref{sec:theory} audits the bound's slack empirically
instead of assuming it away, and the method's empirical justification is the ablations in
Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}. Prior work in this journal addresses adjacent
aspects --- gradient-based task organization~\cite{huang2026gradtaskorg}, gradient-norm
regularization for dissimilar tasks~\cite{wang2026gnr}, and task-adaptive
routing~\cite{pan2026prototype} --- but none of it derives the protected directions from a
forgetting bound, which is the specific step taken here. FOLoRA is constructed to fill that gap,
and because it weights directions by Fisher eigenvalues it contains uniform
(subspace-orthogonal) protection as the special case in which all eigenvalues are equal
(Sec.~\ref{sec:theory}).
