
\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
We use a ViT-B/16 backbone pre-trained on ImageNet-1k, frozen throughout, with LoRA
adapters (rank $r=16$, $\alpha=16$) attached to the MLP down-projection of every
transformer block (12 adapters in total). The classifier head is a single linear layer
grown as new classes arrive. All methods share the identical backbone and head; they
differ only in the adapter regularization.

\textbf{Datasets.} Split CIFAR-100 (20 tasks $\times$ 5 classes) and Split ImageNet-R
(20 tasks $\times$ 10 classes), both under the class-incremental protocol (task identity
unavailable at test time).

\textbf{Baselines.} (i) \emph{Seq-LoRA}: sequential fine-tuning of the shared adapter
with no protection (lower bound); (ii) \emph{EWC-LoRA}: diagonal Fisher on the LoRA
parameters~\cite{kirkpatrick2017overcoming}; (iii) \emph{O-LoRA}: per-task adapters with
orthogonal initialization~\cite{wang2023olora}; (iv) \emph{L2P}: a pool of prompts where the
prompt is selected per input by key--query matching~\cite{wang2022l2p}; (v) \emph{CODA-Prompt}: a set
of prompt components combined by attention~\cite{smith2023coda}; (vi) \emph{InfLoRA}: a
per-task subspace projected to interfere minimally with the previous
ones~\cite{liang2024inflora}; (vii) \emph{FOLoRA} (ours).

\textbf{Training.} AdamW with learning rate $10^{-3}$, batch size 32, and bf16 mixed
precision. We train the adapter-based methods (Seq-LoRA, EWC-LoRA, O-LoRA, InfLoRA,
FOLoRA) for $5$ epochs per task --- for the Fisher-weighted ones this avoids over-fitting
and keeps the empirical Fisher meaningful (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}), and it places the
whole adapter family on one budget --- the prompt-based methods (L2P, CODA-Prompt) are
trained for $20$ epochs per task, their optimal setting since their key-based prompt
selection benefits from more epochs. Prompt geometry is a substantive choice rather than a detail
--- CODA-Prompt's contribution is a \emph{large} pool of components combined by attention
--- so we use each prompt method's own geometry (L2P: pool $20$, length $5$;
CODA-Prompt: pool $100$, length $8$) and report CODA-Prompt at its faithful geometry.
We also ran CODA-Prompt with L2P's geometry (pool $20$, length $5$) at $20$ epochs; over
the three seeds the two configurations share it scores $75.83\pm0.29$ on CIFAR-100 against
$74.87\pm0.47$ for the faithful geometry over those same three ($74.71\pm0.71$ over its
full five). We report the faithful
configuration because it is the geometry CODA-Prompt specifies. We state plainly that this
is also the configuration in which CODA-Prompt scores the \emph{lower} of the two numbers we
measured, so FOLoRA's margin against it is correspondingly larger than it would be against
the alternative $75.83$; the choice is justified by fidelity to the method, not by the
margin it produces.
For FOLoRA we fix the main configuration by a grid search and then do not retune it:
$\lambda\in\{1,3,10\}$ at $k{=}64$ and $k\in\{16,64,128\}$ at $\lambda{=}3$, plus
$\lambda{=}0$ (penalty removed, everything else unchanged) as the ablation control. The
main configuration $k{=}64$, $\lambda{=}3$ was fixed \emph{before} the extra seeds of the
top-up landed and is reported as such throughout; $\lambda{=}10$ is not distinguishable
from it on the five seeds the two share ($\Delta=+0.08$, $p=0.887$,
Table~\ref{tab:ablation}) and appears as a grid point there, not as the main configuration. The importance kernel is estimated
from up to 300 per-sample gradients per task. EWC's regularization strength is likewise
tuned on CIFAR-100: $\lambda\in\{1,3,10,30\}$ at five seeds, and a wider grid
$\lambda\in\{100,300,1000,3000\}$ at ten seeds that was run on both benchmarks
($\lambda{=}3000$ was not run on ImageNet-R). In Table~\ref{tab:main} we report each
benchmark's \emph{best-scoring} EWC point from that grid --- $\lambda{=}300$ on CIFAR-100 and
$\lambda{=}1000$ on ImageNet-R --- i.e.\ we deliberately take the strongest configuration of
the baseline \emph{on each benchmark}, and we report the whole curve, including the
pre-registered baseline $\lambda{=}100$, in Table~\ref{tab:ewc-lambda}.
Selecting these strengths is itself an optimization problem, and here it is solved
exhaustively: every point we compare against is an actual run whose configuration file is
in the repository.
Two properties of that selection belong here rather than in a footnote. First, it is
\emph{test-set} selection: the EWC-LoRA operating point we report on a benchmark is the
point of its grid that scores highest \emph{on that benchmark's test set}, which is the
strongest version of the baseline and the one least favourable to us, but it is still a
choice informed by the test data, and no held-out split was used to make it. Second, the
two methods are chosen differently: EWC-LoRA is re-tuned per benchmark, whereas FOLoRA's
single $(\lambda,k)$ is fixed on CIFAR-100 and then carried to ImageNet-R unchanged. The
asymmetry works against us on ImageNet-R and in favour of the baseline there, and we state
it so that the transfer claim in the abstract is read as what it is --- one setting applied
without retuning, not a setting validated on both benchmarks before the fact. Derivative-free and metaheuristic optimizers are the usual alternative
when the search space is too large to enumerate~\cite{alhussan2026berpo,elkenawy2026gso,barshandeh2026graylangurs,alharbi2026ninja,eid2026smoa};
we do not use one, because a search whose trajectory cannot be enumerated would make the
baseline tuning above harder to audit rather than easier.

\textbf{Metrics and evaluation protocol.} We evaluate every method under a common
frozen-feature nearest-class-mean (NCM) protocol: after the final task we L2-normalize the
CLS feature of the adapted backbone and classify by the nearest class mean computed from
the training features of each task seen so far, with each task's means computed from that
task's own training split. The protocol is standard in this
literature~\cite{wang2022l2p,smith2023coda,liang2024inflora}, but it is \emph{not} the one
under which those methods are reported in their original papers --- L2P, CODA-Prompt and
InfLoRA each report a classifier learned jointly with their prompts --- so every
prompt-based and adapter-based row in Table~\ref{tab:main} is a \emph{re-evaluation} of
that method by us under one decision rule, not a reproduction of its published number. We
adopt the common rule because it removes the classifier head from the measurement: at the
end of a twenty-task sequence the jointly trained head retains only about an eighth of what
the frozen features support --- $7.2$--$9.2\%$ against $1\%$ chance on CIFAR-100, which is
$10$--$13\%$ of the same model's NCM accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:head-acc}) --- so a head-based
comparison would largely measure head drift rather than representation quality. Two consequences should be read alongside the numbers. First, the
class means are recomputed at evaluation time from each task's training split, so the
protocol assumes \emph{evaluation-time} access to past training data. No gradient is ever
computed on a previous task --- this is not training-time replay --- but it is an
assumption, and we return to it in Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}.
Because the assumption is load-bearing, we also measured what it buys. In a strictly
sequential variant the class means are computed and frozen at the end of the task that
produced them, so no past training set is read at evaluation; on the same three seeds the
strict protocol costs $8.5$ points for FOLoRA ($69.43\pm6.83$ against $77.96$ for the same
runs under the standard rule), $14.0$ for EWC-LoRA ($64.40\pm5.69$ against $78.42$) and
$26.8$ for Seq-LoRA ($42.41\pm6.17$ against $69.17$). The two protocols rank the methods
the same way, and the gap is largest where the representation drifts most; Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}
states this in full rather than leaving the standard-rule numbers to be read as
replay-free. Second, because the decision
rule is applied uniformly, the rows are comparable to each other but not to the published
literature; the learned-head figures are given in Table~\ref{tab:head-acc} for reference.
We report average accuracy
over all tasks after the final task ($\overline{\mathrm{ACC}}$) and the CIL--TIL gap
($\mathrm{GAP}$). $\mathrm{GAP}$ is the mean drop, in
points, when a task's test set is classified against all seen classes rather than against
that task's own classes, under the same final-model feature extraction: it measures how
far a method's decision rule is from relying on task identity, and lower is better.
Because every method is run on the same seed set---and therefore sees the same class
order and initialization---we assess the significance of differences between methods with
a paired $t$-test over the seeds common to both. Seed budgets are \emph{not} equal across
methods and we do not pool them: EWC-LoRA and FOLoRA have $n=10$ on both benchmarks;
Seq-LoRA and O-LoRA $n=10$ on CIFAR-100 and $n=5$ on ImageNet-R; InfLoRA $5$/$3$; L2P
$5$/$5$; CODA-Prompt $5$/$3$. SimpleCIL is a deterministic floor ($n=1$, no variance) and
is not tested. Every $p$-value below states the common-seed count it was computed on, and
Table~\ref{tab:main} repeats the budget for each method.

\textbf{Two exemplar-free methods are adjacent enough to warrant a word.} FeCAM
\cite{goswami2023fecam} and EFC \cite{magistri2024efc} are among the closest exemplar-free
continual learners to the setting studied here, and neither is a baseline of
Table~\ref{tab:main} --- for reasons that are properties of our protocol rather than
judgements of their quality. FeCAM contributes a \emph{classifier}: it fits anisotropic
class prototypes to frozen features, which is exactly the regime our NCM head occupies, so
adding it would replace the evaluation rule that every row currently shares and would
therefore measure a classifier rather than the training-time regularizer this paper varies.
EFC contributes a mechanism that requires the backbone \emph{not} to be frozen --- its
feature-drift regularizer exists to hold a moving backbone's features stable --- so running
it under frozen features would delete the mechanism under test. Holding the classifier and
the backbone fixed across every method is what makes the comparisons in
Table~\ref{tab:main} isolate the training procedure; we accordingly claim nothing against
FeCAM or EFC as complete systems, and comparing against either would call for a different
experimental design than the one used here.
Significance is reported on Holm--Bonferroni corrected $p$-values, with the uncorrected
value given alongside whenever a claim turns on the difference. Table~\ref{tab:main} fixes
the confirmatory family, because it asks one question per baseline: within a benchmark and
a metric, the contrasts against the nine CIFAR-100 and eight ImageNet-R baselines are one
family, adjusted step-down over the sorted $p$-values. We do not pool the two benchmarks
into a single family of seventeen tests. They answer different questions---on CIFAR-100 the
comparison is to a baseline at parity, on ImageNet-R it is not---and pooling would inflate
the family to buy protection against an error mode that neither table introduces.
Elsewhere---the EWC $\lambda$ grid of Table~\ref{tab:ewc-lambda}, the ablations of
Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}, the O-LoRA variants of Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}---the comparison
is exploratory and the family is stated where the number is used. The correction removes no
claim the argument rests on. The verdict pre-registered before any seed was run, ImageNet-R
against EWC-LoRA at $\lambda=100$, survives at $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0028$ on the
CIL--TIL gap and
$0.0011$ on average accuracy; its CIFAR-100 counterpart was never claimed as significant and
remains so ($p=0.0734$, corrected $0.2934$); and the paper's largest effect, the
$\lambda{=}0$ ablation, survives a family more than three times larger than the confirmatory
one: on average accuracy $p=0.0005$, corrected $0.012$, across the $32$ CIFAR-100
configurations that support a paired test ($p=0.0004$, corrected $0.011$, on the gap
metric). What the
correction does remove are marginal forgetting differences against L2P, CODA-Prompt and the
weaker EWC-LoRA operating points ($0.023\le p\le0.040$); none is claimed below, and each is
named where it arises. The correction now admits one forgetting contrast it previously did
not, against O-LoRA, which runs in FOLoRA's favour once every per-task adapter is evaluated.

The protocol-independent facts hold across both tables: all methods share one frozen
backbone and one classifier head. What separates the families is the per-task budget:
FOLoRA, Seq-LoRA, EWC-LoRA, L2P and CODA-Prompt reuse one fixed set of trainable
parameters across tasks, whereas O-LoRA and InfLoRA allocate a new adapter per task, so
their \emph{allocated} state grows linearly with the number of tasks
(Table~\ref{tab:resources}, frozen and cumulative columns). The distinction matters because
the two are often conflated: O-LoRA's growth is entirely in frozen state --- at $T{=}20$ it
trains exactly the same $814{,}180$ parameters as FOLoRA --- and InfLoRA trains fewer still
($224{,}356$), because it designs the down-projection $A$ instead of learning it. The
per-task advantage FOLoRA claims over these two is therefore one of \emph{footprint}, not of
trainable parameter count. The classifier head grows with the number of classes seen for
\emph{every} method, because the class-incremental protocol requires it and all methods
share it; it is trained but discarded at evaluation under the NCM protocol, which is why the
trainable column rises between $T{=}1$ and $T{=}20$ for every row.
Fig.~\ref{fig:curve-cifar} and Fig.~\ref{fig:curve-inr} (\ref{app:curves}) take the same final representations
and re-score them as the candidate class set grows; we introduce them together with the
semantics of that axis, because it is easy to misread as a training trajectory. Each figure
shows the candidate-set restriction curves that the corresponding column of
Table~\ref{tab:main} collapses into a single final-task average.
Both figures re-score a \emph{single} feature extraction of the terminal model: the
horizontal axis is the number of candidate classes the nearest-class-mean decision must
choose among (5 per task on CIFAR-100, 10 on ImageNet-R, up to 100 and 200 respectively),
\emph{not} elapsed training and \emph{not} the state of the model at that point of the
sequence. The leftmost point therefore already contains the forgetting accumulated over the
whole sequence, and the curve shows what happens to one fixed representation as the
candidate set grows. On
CIFAR-100 all six curves lie within five points of each other at the smallest candidate set
($93.6$ to $98.2$) and span $64.9$ to $78.0$ at the full one; FOLoRA and EWC-LoRA in
particular are indistinguishable at every candidate set, their paired difference ranging
over only $0.66$ points and never exceeding $0.46$ in either direction. On ImageNet-R the
spread at the smallest candidate set is already $7.8$ points ($82.9$ for CODA-Prompt to
$90.7$ for EWC-LoRA, with FOLoRA at $90.5$ and O-LoRA at $83.2$), and FOLoRA's difference
from EWC-LoRA stays small as the candidate set grows rather than widening: $-0.19$ at the
smallest set, $-1.12$ at the fifth task's set, $+0.04$ at the full set, a spread of $1.15$
points --- which is why we do not read that accuracy difference as an advantage that
compounds with the number of tasks.

\textbf{FOLoRA matches the strongest regularized baseline; it does not beat it.} Under the
comparison fixed in advance --- a paired $t$-test against EWC-LoRA at $\lambda=100$ ---
FOLoRA is ahead by $+0.65$ points on CIFAR-100 ($p=0.0734$, which the pre-registered rule
calls ``matches or slightly exceeds'' and \emph{not} ``outperforms'') and by $+2.85$ points
on ImageNet-R ($p=0.0002$, all ten per-seed differences positive), which the same rule
calls a significant lead. We report that verdict, and also why it must not be read as a
general advantage: $\lambda$ is a hyperparameter of the \emph{baseline}, and the comparison
moves with it. On CIFAR-100 EWC-LoRA's four tuned points span $77.22$ to $78.00$ while
FOLoRA scores $77.87$ --- inside that span --- with paired differences of $-0.14$
($p=0.644$) at $\lambda=300$, $+0.21$ ($p=0.496$) at $\lambda=1000$ and $+0.47$
($p=0.251$) at $\lambda=3000$. That is parity at every CIFAR-100 point, and EWC-LoRA's best
point sits $0.13$ points \emph{above} FOLoRA. On ImageNet-R the same structure appears with
a wider spread, and the lead is confined to one end of it: $\lambda=100$ is that benchmark's
weakest EWC point at $63.52$, and it is there --- and only there --- that the comparison
reaches significance, while at $\lambda=300$ and $\lambda=1000$, both roughly $2.8$ points
higher, the difference collapses to $+0.05$ ($p=0.9010$) and $+0.04$ ($p=0.9235$). The sign of our
difference against EWC-LoRA is therefore set by which operating point of the baseline is
read, so we report the whole curve (Table~\ref{tab:ewc-lambda}) rather than the point that
reads best for us. EWC-LoRA was not run at $\lambda=3000$ on ImageNet-R.

\textbf{On the CIL--TIL gap the comparison is mixed, and we report both directions.} The
direction is set by the baseline's regularization strength, not by the method. On CIFAR-100
EWC-LoRA's gap is the smaller one at the two larger strengths, but neither difference
survives the correction of Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}: the paired gap is $+0.32$ points at
$\lambda=1000$ and $+0.39$ at $\lambda=3000$, nominally significant at $p=0.0400$ and
$0.0401$ and corrected to $0.16$ in both cases, so on this benchmark the two methods are
indistinguishable in the gap direction. At $\lambda=300$ the difference is $+0.07$
($p=0.568$) and at the pre-registered $\lambda=100$ it is $-0.30$ ($p=0.071$), where the
sign runs the other way without reaching significance either way. On ImageNet-R the
direction reverses and there the difference survives: FOLoRA has the significantly
\emph{smaller} gap at $\lambda=100$ ($-1.17$ points, $p=0.0004$, corrected $0.0028$). At
$\lambda=300$ the nominal advantage ($-0.41$, $p=0.0317$) does not survive correction
($0.13$), and at $\lambda=1000$ the two are indistinguishable ($+0.27$, $p=0.203$).
Because average accuracy and the gap are measured under the same
final feature space---a representation that separates the classes better both classifies
better and loses less when the candidate set grows---the two co-vary across these methods.
We therefore do not treat the gap as an independent axis of merit in either direction; we
report it rather than leaving a reader to assume the favourable direction.

\textbf{FOLoRA is ahead of the parameter-efficient family, and there the difference is
uniform.} Against L2P, CODA-Prompt, InfLoRA and O-LoRA the paired differences in average
accuracy are significant on both benchmarks with every per-seed difference of the same
sign: across the four baselines the mean paired difference runs from $+3.61$ to $+12.92$
points on CIFAR-100 (corrected $p\le0.008$) and from $+7.45$ to $+13.20$ on ImageNet-R
(corrected $p\le0.007$). The upper end of each range is set by the initialization-only
O-LoRA row, and it narrows against the training-time orthogonalized objective that the
original paper specifies, which we also ran: on CIFAR-100 to $+7.10$ and $+5.60$ at the two
constraint strengths ($\lambda_1{=}0.1$ and $\lambda_1{=}1.0$; corrected $p<0.001$ for both
within the two-member family those variants form), and on ImageNet-R to $+10.59$
and $+5.74$ (corrected $p=0.034$ each in the same family). This is the paper's positive
empirical result. It is also the comparison in which the baselines' seed budgets are
smallest ($n=5$, or $n=3$ for CODA-Prompt and InfLoRA on ImageNet-R), which is why
Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} lists each budget separately instead of implying a shared one --- and
why the claim is stated against the parameter-efficient family rather than against
full-parameter regularization. Under the NCM rule used throughout this table two rows sit below
the frozen-feature floor of $70.31$: O-LoRA at its initialization-only setting on CIFAR-100
($64.94$) and Seq-LoRA ($69.31$, unprotected sequential training of the shared adapter). Only
the first is decisive. O-LoRA's gap is $5.4$ points and is significant against the constant
floor ($p=0.0003$, one-sample test over ten seeds); Seq-LoRA's is $1.0$ point and is not
($p=0.13$). We therefore do not rest an argument on Seq-LoRA's shortfall --- it is a nominal
sign, not evidence --- and the claim that the forgetting this paper targets lives in the
adapter rather than in the head is carried by the direct temporal measurement of
Sec.~\ref{sec:forgetting}, not by this comparison. Sec.~\ref{sec:posthoc} measures how much of
the floor comparison depends on the decision rule: under a stronger training-free head neither
shortfall is detectable, and the ordering between the two strongest rows does not change.
Every other
row clears the floor: InfLoRA at $72.95$ on CIFAR-100 and $58.93$ on ImageNet-R, O-LoRA at
$53.72$ on ImageNet-R, against floors of $70.31$ and $49.71$ respectively; and the
training-time orthogonality term lifts the O-LoRA mean above the CIFAR-100 floor at both
constraint strengths ($71.21$ and $72.72$); Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}
reports those configurations in full.

\subsection{How much of the ranking is the decision rule?}
\label{sec:posthoc}

Every number in Table~\ref{tab:main} is produced by one decision rule --- cosine
nearest-class-mean on terminal features --- and it is fair to ask whether the ordering is a
property of the representations or of the rule. Table~\ref{tab:posthoc} separates the two by
re-scoring the \emph{same} features with RanPAC~\cite{mcdonnell2023ranpac}, a training-free
ridge classifier over $M{=}10{,}000$ random projections whose regularization is selected per
task on a held-out split of that task's training features. Nothing is retrained and no adapter
hyperparameter changes: the two columns of a row come from a single checkpoint, and the NCM
column is recomputed from the same cached features rather than quoted from
Table~\ref{tab:main}. Before a RanPAC number is accepted we verify that the cached features
reproduce the published NCM accuracy of that exact run; all rows reproduce to the second
decimal, which rules out the silent failure mode in which an adapter is not fully restored and
the evaluation quietly falls back to the frozen backbone.

\input{tables/tab_posthoc}

The decision rule dominates the absolute level on both benchmarks: every configuration gains
between $5.3$ and $13.7$ points, and the frozen backbone alone reaches $81.57$ on CIFAR-100 and
$60.00$ on ImageNet-R. On the same checkpoints, that is above FOLoRA's NCM figure of $77.83$ on
CIFAR-100 but $6.4$ points below its $66.43$ on ImageNet-R, so a stronger head does not by itself
overtake adaptation on the harder benchmark.
The gain is smallest for the two strongest adapters ($5.3$ and $7.2$
points for FOLoRA, $5.3$ and $7.6$ for the tuned EWC-LoRA) and largest for the weakest ($9.9$
and $13.0$ for O-LoRA, and $13.7$ for Seq-LoRA on CIFAR-100): the stronger head compresses the
differences among the methods that already sit at the top rather than creating them.

What survives the swap is the identity of the top two rows and FOLoRA's standing within the
parameter-efficient family. Measured over three class orders on both benchmarks, FOLoRA and the
tuned EWC-LoRA are indistinguishable under RanPAC on both: $84.98$ against $85.04$ on CIFAR-100
(paired difference $-0.06\pm0.32$, $p=0.78$) and $71.74$ against $72.15$ on ImageNet-R
($-0.41\pm0.79$, $p=0.46$). This is the same verdict the cosine rule gives on the ten-seed means
($-0.14$, $p=0.64$ on CIFAR-100; $+0.04$, $p=0.92$ on ImageNet-R), so the parity between the two
strongest methods is a property of the representations and not of the decision rule. Against the
rest of the parameter-efficient family FOLoRA is ahead under RanPAC on both benchmarks, by
$+2.44$ over InfLoRA on CIFAR-100 and by $+5.01$ over Seq-LoRA on ImageNet-R, both at the narrow
end and both at $p<0.01$. What the adapter buys is therefore not an artefact of the cosine rule:
FOLoRA plus RanPAC is $3.41$ points above the frozen backbone on CIFAR-100 and $11.74$ on
ImageNet-R.

One secondary comparison is weaker than we first wrote it, and we give the corrected form
rather than the version we would prefer. Under NCM, O-LoRA at its initialization-only setting
is $5.4$ points below the frozen-feature floor and Seq-LoRA $1.0$ point below it, of which only
the first is significant; reading the second as ``sequential adaptation ends below simply
classifying frozen features'' does not survive a test against the floor ($p=0.13$, ten seeds).
Under RanPAC the picture dissolves in both directions: the frozen backbone reaches $81.57$, the
three remaining adapted backbones lie within one point of it (Seq-LoRA $81.95$, O-LoRA $80.80$,
InfLoRA $82.54$) against a run-to-run standard deviation of $0.89$ points for the difference of
two runs --- InfLoRA sits at the edge of that band, $0.08$ points beyond it --- and only the two
strongest adapted rows rise clearly above it (EWC-LoRA
$85.04$ and FOLoRA $84.98$, $+3.5$ and $+3.4$). The floor therefore separates nothing in the
middle of the table once the rule changes. ImageNet-R has no such ambiguity to resolve: under NCM every adapted backbone
already clears the floor ($49.71$) by at least $4.0$ points (ten-seed means,
Table~\ref{tab:main}), and under RanPAC by at least $4.5$ ($64.53$ to $72.15$ against
$60.00$), so the floor discussion is a CIFAR-100 question
on either rule. What carries the paper is the direct temporal measurement of
Sec.~\ref{sec:forgetting}: Seq-LoRA falls $21.71$ points below its own per-task accuracy and
FOLoRA $10.39$, and that gap does not depend on which classifier reads the features.

We also do not claim that Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the best achievable number on these
benchmarks. Read with the same head, the frozen backbone reaches $81.57$ on CIFAR-100, which only
the two strongest adapted rows clearly beat ($85.04$ and $84.98$; InfLoRA's $82.54$ sits at the
edge of the noise band and Seq-LoRA's $81.95$ inside it), and on ImageNet-R all five adapted rows
beat its $60.00$. So
adaptation is not redundant, and a practitioner whose only objective is accuracy on CIFAR-100 may
still prefer the frozen backbone with a trained-nowhere classifier to most of the PEFT-CL rows.
What Table~\ref{tab:main} measures is what adaptation buys under a common, parameter-free
decision rule, which is the question this paper is about.

\subsection{True temporal forgetting}
\label{sec:forgetting}

The second column of Table~\ref{tab:main} is a CIL--TIL gap, not a forgetting measure. As set out
in Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} it is computed from a single feature extraction of the \emph{terminal}
model re-scored against candidate sets of increasing size, so it cannot separate ``the model
forgot task $j$'' from ``the model was never asked to discriminate task $j$'s classes against the
later ones''. To measure forgetting we re-ran four configurations with a per-boundary protocol:
after each of the $20$ tasks, the features of every test image are extracted from the model
\emph{as it stands at that boundary} and scored by NCM over the classes seen so far. This yields
a full $T\times T$ matrix $A$ whose entry $A_{t,j}$ is the accuracy on task $j$'s test set after
learning task $t$, and from it
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BWT} = \frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{j<T-1}\!\bigl(A_{T-1,j}-A_{j,j}\bigr),
\qquad
\mathrm{PD} = \frac{1}{T}\sum_{j}\Bigl(\max_{t\ge j}A_{t,j}-A_{T-1,j}\Bigr),
\end{equation}
both in points, negative BWT meaning forgetting. BWT is the standard definition; the peak-drop
measure PD adds a penalty when a task's accuracy falls after it was learned and later recovers,
which BWT on its own can hide. Table~\ref{tab:bwt} reports both, together with the average
incremental accuracy (AIA, the mean over boundaries of the accuracy the model then had on every
class seen so far) and the final accuracy of the same runs, so that forgetting is not read in
isolation from what the method achieves nor from the trajectory that produced it.

\input{tables/tab_bwt}

Four findings. First, the method roughly halves the forgetting of unprotected sequential
training: BWT improves by $11.33$ points ($p=0.003$ paired over three seeds) and FOLoRA also
ends $8.74$ points higher on final accuracy ($p=0.012$). Second, FOLoRA is \emph{not} the
best-forgetting method in the table. Against the tuned EWC-LoRA of Table~\ref{tab:main}
($\lambda{=}300$) neither its BWT ($-1.64$, $p=0.18$) nor its final accuracy ($-0.63$,
$p=0.47$) differs significantly, and against the more strongly regularized $\lambda{=}1000$
variant it forgets significantly more ($-2.65$, $p=0.013$, uncorrected, $n=3$). We read this as
the parity established in Sec.~\ref{sec:main_results} extending to forgetting, not as evidence
that FOLoRA protects memory better than full-parameter regularization. Third, the peak-drop
measure tracks $|\mathrm{BWT}|$ to within $0.11$ points in every row, so none of the four curves
drops after a task is learned and later recovers: the ordering above is not an artefact of a
single definition. Fourth, the trajectory measure agrees with the endpoint. AIA averages over
all $20$ boundaries rather than the last one, so it weights the mid-sequence points that
neither BWT nor final accuracy sees: FOLoRA reaches $84.08\pm1.25$ against $85.22\pm1.32$ for
the tuned EWC-LoRA ($-1.14$, $p=0.28$) and $4.07$ points above Seq-LoRA ($p=0.013$), which is
the same ordering as the other two columns.

\textbf{The penalty carries the entire effect.} Setting $\lambda=0$ removes the
Fisher-weighted orthogonality penalty and changes nothing else; on the five seeds common to
both configurations average accuracy falls from $78.32$ to $69.59$ ($\Delta=+8.72$,
$p=0.0005$), i.e.\ to the level of unprotected sequential fine-tuning ($69.31$,
Table~\ref{tab:main}). (The $\lambda{=}3$ full ten-seed mean is the $77.87$ of
Table~\ref{tab:main}; the two figures differ only in seed budget, not in configuration.)
The benefit of FOLoRA is therefore
attributable to the regularization term rather than to the shared adapter, the rank
constraint or the training recipe --- which is what the derivation predicts: with no
penalty the protected subspace is empty and the method degenerates to Seq-LoRA.

\textbf{The direction of the subspace matters, though three seeds do not establish by how
much.} A penalty on \emph{some} fixed subspace would also restrain the adapter, so we ran the
main configuration with the protected subspace replaced by a random one --- same per-layer
rank $k{=}64$, same strength $\lambda{=}3$, same scale, nothing else changed. Protection
along random directions reaches $74.35\pm1.43$ against $78.37\pm0.84$ for the Fisher
subspace on the three common seeds, a paired difference of $-4.02$ points ($p=0.070$,
$n{=}3$). The sign is the one the method predicts --- a subspace chosen by curvature is
worth roughly four points more than one chosen at random --- but with three seeds and a
paired spread of $1.95$ points it does not reach significance, and we report it as a
direction rather than as a demonstration. What the comparison does rule out is the reading
that the penalty works merely by shrinking the adapter: an equally strong penalty on an
uninformative subspace recovers only part of the effect.

\textbf{At $k{=}64$ the $\lambda$ response is a step, not a peak, over the range we
probed.} Accuracy rises from $69.59$ at $\lambda=0$ to $77.33$ at $\lambda=1$ and $77.87$
at $\lambda=3$, and does not move beyond that: $\lambda=10$ prints $78.24$ on the five
seeds it was run on, but on those same five seeds the main configuration is ahead by
$+0.08$ ($p=0.887$) in the paired test --- the two are
indistinguishable, and the $0.37$-point gap between the printed means is a seed-budget
difference, not a gain. Only
$\lambda=0$ separates from the main configuration ($p=0.0005$); $\lambda\in\{1,10\}$ are
each within noise of it ($p=0.111$ and $p=0.887$). We therefore do not claim an interior
optimum at $k{=}64$ --- the grid does not resolve one, and any ordering among
$\lambda\in\{1,3,10\}$ is inside the run-to-run band of Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}. The
paired tests behind these accuracies are in Table~\ref{tab:ablation}.

\textbf{At $k{=}16$ the decline at large $\lambda$ is visible.} Extending the sweep on the
smaller protected subspace, where the penalty acts on fewer directions, average accuracy
peaks at $\lambda\in\{3,10\}$ ($78.14$ and $77.90$, both $n{=}5$) and then declines
before plateauing: $75.35$ at $\lambda=100$ ($n{=}3$), $73.61$ at $\lambda=300$ ($n{=}10$)
and $73.85$ at $\lambda=1000$ ($n{=}3$) --- the last two sit inside the run-to-run band, so
we read the response as saturating rather than continuing to fall. This is the interior
optimum that
Remark~\ref{rem:regularized} predicts, and it is the reason we treat the large-$\lambda$
end as a real failure mode rather than as a harmless over-regularization. The seed budgets
differ across these points, so we read the shape rather than the individual gaps; the
paired tests behind them are in Table~\ref{tab:appendix-signif-b}.

\textbf{$k$ is a memory--accuracy dial, not a knob to tune.} Accuracies at
$k\in\{16,64,128\}$ differ by less than one point ($78.14$, $77.87$, $77.66$) and no paired
difference is significant ($p\ge0.2768$, the $k{=}128$ comparison; Table~\ref{tab:ablation}).
The same insensitivity holds on the harder benchmark: $67.46\pm0.35$, $66.37\pm0.77$ and
$67.47\pm0.12$ at $k=16$, $64$ and $128$ ($n=3$, $10$ and $3$). Over the three common seeds
the paired differences from the main configuration are $-0.57$ ($p=0.311$) and $-0.58$
($p=0.199$) in the convention of Table~\ref{tab:ablation} (main configuration minus the
setting), i.e.\ the two other ranks are marginally ahead --- again inside the noise, and in
the opposite direction to CIFAR-100, as a difference of this size would be.
The importance state grows linearly in $k$ --- $47.8$\,MiB at $k{=}16$, $182.8$\,MiB
at $k{=}64$ and $362.8$\,MiB at $k{=}128$ on the accounting convention of
Table~\ref{tab:resources} (protected directions plus reference snapshot). We
report $k{=}64$ because it was the pre-registered main configuration, not because it
scores highest --- it does not. Since $k$ buys no measurable accuracy, the practical
recommendation for a memory-constrained device is $k{=}16$ at $\lambda\in\{3,10\}$; rank
selection under a deployment budget has been studied for quantized adapters~\cite{bayattoksoz2026sqlora},
and here the memory is spent on the importance state rather than on the weights.

\textbf{Fisher weighting moves the strength at which the plateau is reached, not the
plateau.} Setting $\sigma_j\equiv1$ and changing nothing else gives a second arm on the
same $\lambda$ grid --- ten seeds at every strength for the equal-weight arm, and for the
weighted arm ten at $\lambda=3$ and five at the others. At small strengths the weighted
arm is clearly ahead: $+3.83$ points at $\lambda=1$ ($p=0.012$, five paired seeds) and
$+4.19$ at $\lambda=3$ ($p=6\cdot10^{-7}$, ten). The gap closes at $\lambda=10$, where the
paired difference is $+0.38$ ($p=0.687$, five seeds); over all ten seeds the equal-weight
arm sits at $76.66\pm2.37$ there, against the weighted arm's $78.24\pm0.64$ over its five
($77.86$ for the equal-weight arm over the same five), so what separates the two at this
strength is spread rather than level. The fourth strength
divides them rather than confirming them: at $\lambda=30$ the weighted arm falls to
$75.02$ while the equal-weight arm reaches $77.93$ over its full ten seeds ($77.81$ over the
five the two share) --- the paired difference is $-2.79$ ($p=0.004$). The weighting therefore buys an earlier and narrower optimum,
not a higher one. The weighted arm is on its plateau by $\lambda=3$ and has left it by
$\lambda=30$, whereas the equal-weight arm climbs monotonically from $73.11$ at
$\lambda=1$ to $77.93$ at $\lambda=30$, arriving at the same plateau somewhere in
$\lambda\in[10,30]$ --- a factor of three to ten further along the axis. Inside the
weighted arm's window the spread is also tighter (sd $0.64$--$1.13$ over
$\lambda\in\{1,3,10\}$ against $1.43$--$2.37$ for the equal-weight arm at the same
strengths). At their own best strengths on this grid --- $\lambda=10$ for the weighted arm
and $\lambda=30$ for the equal-weight arm --- the two are indistinguishable ($+0.43$,
$p=0.44$, five paired seeds). We do not claim --- and Sec.~\ref{sec:equalweight} does not
claim --- that the weighting raises the achievable accuracy over a \emph{well-tuned}
equal-weight penalty, because the weighted family contains the equal-weight geometry as
the case $\sigma_j\equiv1$. The difference our data support lies in the operating point
rather than in the ceiling: one $\lambda$ reaches the plateau for the weighted formulation,
which is the property the same-hyperparameter protocol of Sec.~\ref{sec:main_results} uses
across benchmarks, whereas the equal-weight arm is far below it at $\lambda\le3$ --- and at
the far end the trade reverses, since the weighted formulation is the one that requires
$\lambda$ to stay inside its window. The matched-$\lambda$ pairings are listed in
Table~\ref{tab:appendix-signif-b}, at the common-seed count rather than at either arm's
full budget. The same contrast at the smaller protected rank is much flatter. Over the three
seeds the two arms share, the weighted arm is $77.94$ against $77.17$ at $\lambda=3$
($+0.76$, $p=0.24$) and $77.63$ against $77.99$ at $\lambda=10$ ($-0.36$, $p=0.0099$); over
its full five seeds the weighted arm is $78.14\pm0.68$ and $77.90\pm0.71$ at the two
strengths. The ordering changes sign between $\lambda=3$ and $\lambda=10$, and the
$\lambda=10$ difference --- statistically detectable before correction, but less than half
the $0.89$-point resolution of the protocol --- is the same conclusion the $k{=}64$ grid
reaches, and the reason we do not read either sign as an accuracy claim.

\subsection{Limitations and discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Reproducibility floor}
We measured the run-to-run spread directly rather than inferring it from a single pair of
runs. On CIFAR-100, at seed~0 and with a fixed configuration, we ran the EWC-LoRA
configuration seven times and the FOLoRA configuration four times; the \texttt{config.json}
files of each group agree field by field except for the \texttt{tag}, and the one other
field that varies (\texttt{folora\_lambda}) is not read by the EWC method at all, which we
confirmed in the source. The single-run standard deviation is $0.69$ points for EWC-LoRA
(range $75.97$--$78.06$ over seven runs) and $0.47$ for FOLoRA (range $77.22$--$78.37$).
The two are of the same order (variance ratio $F{=}2.16$, $p{=}0.56$), so we pool them:
$s{=}0.63$ over eleven runs. A difference between two independent runs therefore has a
standard deviation of $\sqrt{2}\,s = 0.89$ points, and that is the band drawn in
Fig.~\ref{fig:curve-cifar}.
Two consequences follow. First, the $1.29$-point gap we quoted in an earlier version of this
section --- two runs at the same seed that produced $77.26$ and $75.97$ --- is $2.1\,s$, not the
floor: it is one pair out of the sample, and the two most extreme of the seven runs lie further
apart still ($78.06$ and $75.97$, $3.3\,s$). With a single pair there is no way to tell a floor
from a tail, which is why we replaced the pair with repeated runs. Second, and this governs how the table should be read, the differences
that decide the ordering of the \emph{leading} rows are far inside the band: FOLoRA against
tuned EWC-LoRA differs by $0.13$ points on CIFAR-100, and either of them against InfLoRA by
at least $4.9$ points. A single seed, or any subset of seeds chosen after the fact, therefore
cannot support a claim in either direction at that end of the table. This is why we report
paired $t$-tests over seeds throughout and why we describe any difference inside the band as
parity rather than as a win --- including differences that happen to favor us.

\paragraph{Equivalence, not merely non-significance}
A paired $t$-test that fails to reject is not evidence of equality, so for the one comparison
whose conclusion is \emph{parity} --- FOLoRA against tuned EWC-LoRA --- we run a two
one-sided test (TOST) instead of relying on a non-significant difference. The margin is
$\delta{=}1.0$ points, set at the resolution of a single comparison: it is $1.1$ times the
$0.89$-point standard deviation of the difference of two independent runs measured above, so
a smaller margin would claim an equivalence that no number of seeds could establish.
Equivalence is established at both tuned EWC-LoRA points on both benchmarks --- on CIFAR-100
$-0.14$ against $\lambda{=}300$ ($p_{\mathrm{TOST}}{=}0.008$) and $+0.21$ against
$\lambda{=}1000$ ($p{=}0.014$); on ImageNet-R $+0.05$ ($p{=}0.015$) and $+0.04$
($p{=}0.017$) --- and it is \emph{not} established at the pre-registered $\lambda{=}100$ on
ImageNet-R ($+2.85$, $p\approx1$), which is the same setting where the accuracy difference is
significant after correction; there the text reports a difference rather than parity.

\paragraph{Where the variance comes from}
On CIFAR-100 the accuracy of FOLoRA is consistent in mean but noisier across seeds than
the regularized baselines it is compared against --- $\pm0.90$ for FOLoRA against $\pm0.54$
for EWC-LoRA and $\pm0.64$ for InfLoRA (Table~\ref{tab:main}) --- while being
\emph{less} noisy than unprotected sequential training ($\pm1.92$ for Seq-LoRA and
$\pm3.03$ for O-LoRA at its initialization-only setting), which is consistent with the
penalty damping seed-to-seed drift rather than amplifying it. We attribute the residual
variance to two sources. First, the importance kernel is estimated
from a finite per-sample budget (up to 300 gradients per task), and its top-$k$
approximation is sensitive to the particular samples that land in the tails of the
estimate. Second, under a frozen backbone, a fixed-rank ($r{=}16$) adapter and no replay,
the class ordering exerts a strong effect on the final subspace, so individual seeds drift
apart. We further found that the epoch budget interacts with the method family:
adapter-based Fisher methods degrade under longer training because over-fitting drives the
per-sample gradients toward zero and thereby degenerates the estimated Fisher, whereas
prompt-based methods benefit from longer training of their prompt keys. This motivates the
per-family epoch budgets in Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}. A natural direction for future work is a
stochastic (Monte-Carlo) Fisher estimate and class-order averaging to tighten the
variance.

\paragraph{What we claim, in two layers}
Because the CIFAR effect sizes sit inside the noise band above, we separate the claims into
one layer the evidence supports unconditionally and one it supports only with an explicit
caveat.

The first layer is about \emph{what the method does}, and does not depend on beating
anyone. (i) Removing the Fisher-weighted penalty and changing nothing else returns the
method to the level of unprotected sequential fine-tuning, so the entire effect of FOLoRA
is carried by the regularization term it adds rather than by the shared adapter or the
training recipe (Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}). (ii) The protected rank is not a sensitive
knob: accuracies at $k\in\{16,64,128\}$ differ by less than one point and none of the
differences is significant, while the importance state grows linearly in $k$ --- which
makes $k$ a memory--accuracy dial rather than a hyperparameter to tune. (iii) On CIFAR-100
FOLoRA reaches accuracy \emph{on par with the strongest regularized baseline} rather than
above it; we state parity as parity. Against EWC-LoRA, the comparison flips sign depending
on which of the tuned EWC operating points is taken as the baseline, so we report all of
them in Table~\ref{tab:ewc-lambda} and let the reader see the flip rather than selecting the
point that reads best.

The second layer is where FOLoRA is \emph{ahead}, and it holds against the parameter-efficient
family rather than against full-parameter regularization: against L2P, CODA-Prompt,
InfLoRA and O-LoRA, the paired differences are significant on both benchmarks and
seed-wise all of one sign. That claim is the one we treat as the paper's positive result;
it is also the one where the baseline's seed budget is smallest, so
Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} records the seed sets explicitly for each family instead of implying a
single shared one.

\paragraph{Cost: trainable parameters are traded for importance state}
FOLoRA's efficiency claim is narrower than ``memory-efficient'', and we quantify the
trade-off rather than leaving it implicit. The trainable adapter is the same one at every
task --- it is reused and re-projected --- so the only growth in the trainable column is the
classifier head, which adds the same $0.073$M to every method in Table~\ref{tab:resources}
($0.741$M at $T{=}1$ against $0.814$M at $T{=}20$ for FOLoRA), and the low-rank update merges
back into the frozen backbone, so inference costs nothing extra. Against
that, the importance model is a per-layer rank-$k$ subspace rather than EWC's per-parameter
diagonal, and it is larger. Table~\ref{tab:resources} states both sides with a single
accounting convention: the importance column counts \emph{everything a method must keep
resident to compute its penalty}, i.e. the Fisher plus the parameter anchor for EWC, and
the accumulated directions plus the reference snapshot for FOLoRA. On that convention
FOLoRA at $k{=}64$ is $32.5\times$ EWC's importance state; at $k{=}16$ it is $8.5\times$.
This is the price of representing direction correlations, which a diagonal preconditioner
cannot do at any rank, and it is why we report $k$ as a dial rather than claiming a memory
advantage. The \emph{time} cost is much smaller than the memory cost: estimating the
importance kernel adds roughly $5\%$ to the wall-clock cost of an epoch over EWC-LoRA
($28.2$ against $26.8$\,s/epoch, medians over all seeds of the runs in
Table~\ref{tab:main}), so the eigenvalue decomposition and the $300$-sample Fisher estimate
are not the dominant term --- the training pass is. Prompt-based methods are cheaper per
epoch ($16.7$--$18.8$\,s) but are trained for four times as many epochs, and cost more per
task in total ($333$--$375$\,s against $141$\,s for FOLoRA).

Table~\ref{tab:resources} accounts for what each method must keep resident; it does not by
itself say what the training process actually allocates. We therefore measured peak device
memory directly, each method in its own process, on the same twenty-task CIFAR-100 sequence
and with one epoch per task --- the peak is set by the model, the optimizer state and a
single batch, so the epoch count does not enter into it --- and with every method, FOLoRA
included, at the adapter rank of Table~\ref{tab:main} ($r{=}16$). The four baselines peak
between $1{,}977$ and $2{,}436$\,MiB. FOLoRA at its main configuration ($k{=}64$) peaks at
$2{,}598$\,MiB allocated ($4{,}660$\,MiB reserved), which is $162$\,MiB --- $6.6\%$ --- above
the largest baseline and well inside the $8{,}151$\,MiB of dedicated memory the card reports.
The allocator reserves more than that on every method ($2{,}140$--$2{,}908$\,MiB for the
baselines, $4{,}660$\,MiB for FOLoRA): this is a Windows/WDDM device, where reservations can
be backed by shared system memory and are therefore not bounded by the card, so the allocated
figure is the one we compare across methods.
The whole of FOLoRA's excess is the protected subspace. At the first task, before any
subspace exists, its peak is $2{,}410$\,MiB --- the same figure sequential LoRA and EWC-LoRA
reach at that task --- and from the second task on it rises by $187$\,MiB, which is the
$182.8$\,MiB of Table~\ref{tab:resources} plus the $2.8$\,MiB reference snapshot to within
$1.2$\,MiB. Running the same method at $k{=}16$ and changing nothing else holds the same
first-task peak and grows by $50$\,MiB instead ($47.8$\,MiB of state plus the snapshot, again to
within about one MiB), so the $137$\,MiB that separate
the two configurations is the protected rank, not the Fisher estimate --- the estimate is a
per-task cost that does not accumulate. A device that cannot spare the $k{=}64$ state
therefore loses no measurable accuracy by taking $k{=}16$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}) and
recovers that $137$\,MiB.

Inference is where the accounting reverses. A merged adapter is the backbone, so FOLoRA
adds no tokens and no forward pass; the prompt methods extend every input by the number of
prompt tokens they select, $25$ for L2P (prompt length $5$, top-$5$ of a $20$-slot pool) and
$40$ for CODA-Prompt (length $8$, top-$5$ of $100$ components) in the configurations behind
Table~\ref{tab:main}, and each of those tokens passes through the frozen backbone at every
inference. Table~\ref{tab:resources} does not tabulate this column; the counts are computed
from each run's stored configuration.

\paragraph{Scope}
Two limitations bound the generality of everything above. First, the evidence covers two
benchmarks and a single backbone (ViT-B/16); we make no claim about whether the
curvature-weighted subspace transfers to convolutional backbones or to domain-incremental
shifts, where the class-incremental protocol we evaluate under does not directly apply.
Second, the baselines are reproduced under one shared protocol and one shared backbone
rather than at their published settings, which is what makes the comparison internally
fair but also means the numbers are not the numbers reported in the original papers; where
a baseline's own configuration is materially different --- the prompt methods' pool size
and prompt length --- we state both configurations, so that the comparison cannot be read
as a deliberately weakened baseline. Training budgets are set per family rather than per
method (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}), so the adapter-based baselines that
Table~\ref{tab:main} compares against FOLoRA share its budget; we report no baseline row at
a budget we chose to disadvantage it. One gap in that protocol is worth closing rather than
leaving it as an open question: the five-epoch adapter budget is justified by the behaviour
of the \emph{Fisher-weighted} methods, yet it is applied to the non-Fisher adapter baselines
as well. We therefore trained Seq-LoRA, O-LoRA and InfLoRA for twenty epochs too, changing
nothing else, and compared the two budgets on the three seeds they share
(Table~\ref{tab:epoch-budget}). \emph{The longer budget makes all three baselines worse} ---
by $4.60$ points for Seq-LoRA ($p=0.091$), $10.40$ for O-LoRA ($p=0.003$) and $3.28$ for
InfLoRA ($p=0.008$) --- which is the signature of adapter over-fitting rather than of a
budget that was starved: the five-epoch rows in Table~\ref{tab:main} are the stronger of the
two settings for every one of them. The asymmetry the protocol inherits therefore runs in
the direction opposite to the one that would flatter our comparison, and we report both
budgets rather than only the one that helps.

That leaves exactly one tabulated baseline that is \emph{not} at the formulation its own
paper specifies, and we report that exception and its resolution rather than a summary of
the family. At inference we merge every per-task adapter for both O-LoRA and InfLoRA, which
is the setting their papers specify. On ImageNet-R that places both above the floor ($53.72$
for O-LoRA and $58.93$ for InfLoRA, against $49.71$); on CIFAR-100 it places InfLoRA above
it ($72.95$ against $70.31$) while the tabulated O-LoRA row remains below it ($64.94$). That
row implements O-LoRA's orthogonal \emph{initialization} without the training-time objective
the original paper specifies --- the formulation augments the loss with
$\lambda_1\sum_{i<t}\lVert A_t^{\top}A_i\rVert_F^2$ --- so we ran that objective at two
constraint strengths, $\lambda_1{=}0.1$ and $\lambda_1{=}1.0$ ($n{=}5$ on CIFAR-100, $n{=}3$ on
ImageNet-R). It moves the CIFAR-100 mean from $64.94$ to $71.21\pm1.10$ and $72.72\pm0.90$,
and reaches $56.30$ and $61.15$ on ImageNet-R: every one of these means is above the floor.
The margin is small at the weaker constraint --- $0.9$ points on CIFAR-100, not significant
over the five seeds ($p=0.14$), against $2.4$ points at $\lambda_1{=}1.0$ ($p=0.004$).
Against these stronger
configurations FOLoRA's advantage narrows from $+12.92$ to $+7.10$ and $+5.60$ on CIFAR-100,
and from $+13.20$ to $+10.59$ and $+5.74$ on ImageNet-R, and it remains significant at both
strengths after correction within the two-member family these variants form --- so we report
both strengths for each benchmark rather than the one that suits us best, and the narrower
figures are the margin we are entitled to claim. Both baselines are evaluated at the
per-family budget Sec.~\ref{sec:setup} fixes, so no row of Table~\ref{tab:main} is reported
at a budget chosen to disadvantage it. What separates these configurations from FOLoRA is
not the family but what the constraint acts on: an orthogonality penalty placed in the
adapter input space bounds $A_t^{\top}A_i$ and leaves the merged update $\sum_i B_iA_i$
unconstrained, whereas FOLoRA's Fisher-weighted term penalizes the directions in which that
update moves.

\paragraph{Baselines we did not run}
Three adaptation methods a reader may expect to see here are absent, and we name the reason
for each rather than leaving the omission implicit. Only RanPAC~\cite{mcdonnell2023ranpac} is
training-free, which is why it can be attached to a checkpoint we already have
(Table~\ref{tab:posthoc}); the other three are training procedures. EASE trains a distinct
lightweight adapter per task and a prototype-complement classifier that synthesises old-class
features~\cite{zhou2024ease}, which places it in the per-task-module family this paper is
designed to avoid. HiDe-Prompt trains task-specific prompt pools together with a
task-identity-inference module~\cite{wang2023hideprompt}, i.e.\ the prompt family already
represented in Table~\ref{tab:main} by L2P and CODA-Prompt. SLCA's slow learner fine-tunes the
pre-trained backbone on the first task~\cite{zhang2023slca}, so it is undefined on a frozen
backbone and its parameter budget lies outside the one under which we compare. Reproducing any
of the three would mean implementing its own training recipe at its own budget, and none of them
would answer a question the RanPAC control leaves open.

\paragraph{The bound's slack}
Finally, Sec.~\ref{sec:theory} reports that the forgetting bound is not tight at the
operating point we probe: its remainder and its stationarity residual are the same order as
its leading term, and the finite-sample Fisher estimate is not a calibrated surrogate for
the measured curvature. The derivation is therefore used as a statement of \emph{which}
quantity to penalize, and the empirical support for the method is the ablation above rather
than the bound. We prefer to state this explicitly, since the alternative --- presenting a
loose bound as if it were predictive --- is the failure mode that a reader who re-derives
Eq.~\eqref{eq:eigenbound} would find anyway.

\paragraph{What we do not establish}
Seven things are left open, and we name them rather than letting the tables imply more.
(i) \emph{No advantage over a tuned diagonal EWC-LoRA.} At the tuned operating point of each
benchmark the two are indistinguishable, and no regime we tested separates them there. The one
condition in which the difference is significant is ImageNet-R at the pre-registered
$\lambda{=}100$, which is the weakest point of EWC's grid on that benchmark: Sec.~\ref{sec:main_results}
reports the $+2.85$ ($p=0.0002$) lead at that point and states explicitly that it is not a
general advantage, because at $\lambda\in\{300,1000\}$, both roughly $2.8$ points higher, the
difference is $+0.05$ and $+0.04$. The clearest
test is a $50$-task Split CIFAR-100 sequence ($2$ classes per task) that we ran as a
generality check and did not tune for either method: FOLoRA's best point there is
$74.25\pm1.46$ against $74.94\pm0.69$ for EWC-LoRA at $\lambda{=}1000$, a paired difference
of $-0.69$ points ($p=0.26$, $n{=}5$, uncorrected), with the ordering reversing across the
$\lambda$ grid exactly as it does at twenty tasks. Longer sequences therefore do not produce
the separation a curvature-aware penalty might be expected to show.
(ii) \emph{No accuracy advantage over a well-tuned equal-weight penalty.} The eigenvalue
weighting reaches the $\approx78$ plateau at $\lambda=3$ where the equal-weight arm needs
$\lambda\in[10,30]$, and it is steadier across seeds, but at their best-tuned points the two
arms tie (Sec.~\ref{sec:ablation}), and at $\lambda=30$ the equal-weight arm is the stronger
of the two: the weighting narrows the usable window as well as advancing it. What we
establish about the weighting is its derivation,
its place in the family of penalties, and its operating point --- not a higher ceiling.
(iii) \emph{Memory.} At $k{=}64$ the importance state is $32.5\times$ EWC-LoRA's. Where that
memory is not available, the diagonal baseline is the more practical method, and $k{=}16$
($8.5\times$) is the smallest point we tested; we make no claim that FOLoRA is preferable in
that regime.
(iv) \emph{Evaluation-time data access.} The class means are recomputed from each task's
training split at evaluation time (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}). No gradient is ever computed on a
past task, but the assumption is real: a deployment that cannot retain past training data
cannot reproduce these numbers. We measured what it costs to do without: under a strictly
sequential protocol, in which prototypes are frozen at the end of the task that produced
them, FOLoRA loses $8.5$ points ($69.43\pm6.83$ against $77.96$), EWC-LoRA $14.0$
($64.40\pm5.69$ against $78.42$) and Seq-LoRA $26.8$ ($42.41\pm6.17$ against $69.17$), over
three seeds each. The ranking survives, the levels do not, and the numbers in
Table~\ref{tab:main} should be read as requiring the access.
(v) \emph{The classifier head.} The head is trained jointly and discarded at test time. Its
gradients still shape the adapter, and whether training with no head at all --- fitting the
nearest-class-mean rule directly --- would do better is untested here.
(vi) \emph{Task boundaries and backbone.} The method consumes an explicit task boundary, the
evidence covers one backbone and two benchmarks, and LoRA is attached only to the MLP
down-projection. None of the three is varied, and we do not extrapolate past them.
(vii) \emph{No accuracy advantage over a tuned isotropic displacement penalty.} Replacing the
Fisher-weighted orthogonality term by a plain penalty on the adapter displacement (L2-SP),
scaled to the same RMS as the Fisher term and tuned over the same strengths, gives the same
accuracy on CIFAR-100: $78.27\pm0.38$ against $78.37\pm0.84$ at $\lambda=3$
($\Delta=-0.10$, $p=0.79$) and $77.77\pm0.56$ against $77.88\pm0.58$ at $\lambda=10$
($\Delta=-0.11$, $p=0.52$), three common seeds each. What separates the two penalties in our
experiments is the direction each protects, not the accuracy either reaches; whether the
difference between them matters at a longer budget, a larger task count or a backbone with
more interference is not established here.
